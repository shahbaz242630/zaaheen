How to find where AI helps your business

Start with the work, not the tools: map how each team really does its job, time the steps, and score each step on volume, repetition, rules, data and the cost of a mistake. Only the steps that score well become AI projects; the rest get simple automation, a process fix, or are left with your people.

By Zaaheen · Last updated 26 November 2026

Why start with the work, not the tool?

Because the tool is rarely what decides whether AI pays off. The way the work is done decides it. If you start from a product demonstration, you end up looking for a place to put the product. If you start from the work, you find the places where help is needed and then ask what kind of help fits.

The research points the same way. In a survey of 1,000 executives in 59 countries, Boston Consulting Group found that 74% of companies had yet to show tangible value from AI, and that the companies getting value put most of their effort into people and processes, not into the technology (BCG, 24 October 2024).

Closer to home, Deloitte reported in June 2026 that 84% of organisations in its survey of more than 3,200 leaders in 24 countries had not yet redesigned jobs or workflows around AI (Deloitte Middle East, 4 June 2026). The lesson for a business in Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Riyadh or Doha is simple: look at the work before you buy the tool.

How do you map how a team really works?

Sit with the people doing the job and write down what they actually do, step by step, with a time against each step. Not what the procedure manual says, and not what the manager thinks happens. The workarounds, the copying between screens and the waiting for an answer are where most of the time goes, and they rarely appear on paper.

A useful map, which doubles as a simple AI readiness checklist, records for every step:

  • What triggers it. An email arrives, a form is filed, a customer calls.
  • Who does it. The role, not the person's name.
  • How long it takes, and how many times a day or a month it happens.
  • Where the information comes from and where it goes next.
  • Hand-offs and waiting points, where the work sits with someone else.
  • Exceptions, the cases that do not follow the usual path.

Do this for one team at a time: reception, customer service, sales, finance, HR, operations, procurement, IT, risk and compliance. A finance team at month end and a customer service desk on a Sunday morning look very different, so watch on ordinary days and busy ones.

Once the steps are timed, multiply time by volume. A two-minute task done 400 times a month is over thirteen hours of someone's month. A one-hour task done twice a year is not worth changing at all.

Which steps are good candidates for AI?

Steps that read, write, sort or answer, at volume, where the data already exists. Score each step on five things and the candidates stand out:

ScorePoints towards AIPoints away from AI
VolumeHappens many times a dayA few times a month
RepetitionMuch the same each timeDifferent every time
RulesNeeds reading and judgement on language, like an email or a documentFixed rules a form could apply (that is automation, not AI)
DataThe information is already written down and reachableIt lives in someone's head or on paper
Cost of a mistakeLow, or a person checks before it goes outHigh and hard to undo

Typical steps that score well: sorting a shared inbox, drafting replies to common questions, pulling details out of invoices or contracts, summarising a long case for the next person, or answering staff questions about internal policy. Each one is high volume, mostly language, and based on information the business already holds.

People often ask which department should use AI first. The honest answer is whichever team has the most high-scoring hours, and that is usually found by counting, not guessing. It may be customer service or finance; sometimes it is a team nobody thought of.

When is simple automation better than AI?

When the step follows fixed rules and the inputs are clean. A form, a workflow or a link between two systems will do it cheaper and safer than AI, and it gives the same answer every time. Copying a confirmed order from email into the accounts system, sending a reminder three days before a payment is due, or routing a leave request to the right manager: none of these needs AI.

There is a third answer that often matters more than either: fix the process first. If a step has too many exceptions, unclear ownership or missing data, AI on top of it only makes it break faster. Sometimes the best change is agreeing who owns a task, or removing a step that exists only because of an old system.

What should you leave to your people?

Low-volume work, and work that needs expert judgement where a mistake is costly. A difficult conversation with a long-standing client, a decision on a large credit limit, a disciplinary matter in HR: these are rare, they depend on context, and getting one wrong costs far more than the time AI would save.

Leaving work alone is a real result, not a failure of the exercise. Some work should stay with your people, and a good assessment says so. Even where AI does help, decide in advance where a person must stay in charge: who checks the draft before it goes to a customer, and who signs off a payment.

Can you do this yourself, or do you need someone on site?

You can do a first pass yourself, and for a small team it is worth trying. Pick one team, sit with them for a day, time the steps and score them with the table above. You will learn a lot about your own business.

Three things make it harder from the inside. People describe their job to their manager differently from how they do it. Owners are too close to their own processes to see the workarounds. And without an independent view, it is easy to score steps to fit a tool you have already seen in a demonstration.

What else do people ask about finding where AI helps?

How do I know if my business is ready for AI?

If your people spend their days copying, chasing, checking and answering the same questions, there is usually something to find. Readiness also depends on whether the information those steps need is written down and reachable. Map one team's work and you will know quickly; the free call with us gives you an honest view too.

Where can AI help my business first?

In the team with the most hours spent on high-volume reading, writing, sorting and answering. That is found by timing and counting the steps, not by picking the most popular tool. Start with one or two steps where a mistake is cheap or a person checks the result.

What should not use AI?

Rare tasks, tasks with fixed rules that plain automation handles, and expert judgements where a mistake is costly. Steps sitting on a messy process should wait until the process is fixed. Saying no to AI for those steps saves money.
