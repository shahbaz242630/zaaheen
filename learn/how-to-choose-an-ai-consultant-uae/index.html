How to choose an AI consultant in the UAE

Choose an AI consultant who looks at your real work before recommending anything, sells no tools, takes no commissions, and puts the scope and price in writing. A ranking cannot tell you any of that, but five plain questions can.

By Zaaheen · Last updated 26 November 2026

Zaaheen is an independent AI consultancy based in Dubai that assesses businesses of twenty staff or more across the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain and Oman, on site and role by role. We sell and build no tools, so this guide is written from the buyer's side of the table.

Is there an honest list of the best AI consultancies in the UAE?

No, and you should be wary of any page that claims to be one. Most "top AI consulting companies in Dubai" lists are paid placements, directories that rank whoever buys the top slot, or pages a consultancy wrote about itself and put first.

Even an honest list could not answer your real question. Whether a consultant suits you depends on your business: how many teams you have, how your work is done, what data you hold and what you can afford to change. Nobody can rank that from the outside.

So instead of a list, this guide gives you the checks to run yourself, whether you are looking for an AI consultant in Dubai, Abu Dhabi or Riyadh, or anywhere else in the Gulf. The checks are the same everywhere in the GCC.

Why does the choice matter so much for Gulf businesses right now?

Because most Gulf companies already plan to use AI, and far fewer are set up to make it work. Roland Berger's Middle East report of 10 February 2026 found the gap plainly:

In the report's own words: "nearly 80%" of GCC organisations have AI in their strategic plans, "only one-third" have an enterprise-wide data strategy, "fewer than one in three" have the operating model to scale it, and "only 28%" have an AI ethics or compliance board.

The first action the same report recommends is an organisation-wide AI readiness assessment. That makes the person you hire to do it one of the most important choices in the whole project.

The wider research points the same way. Boston Consulting Group found in October 2024 that 74% of companies had yet to show tangible value from AI, and that the leaders put about 70% of their effort into people and processes rather than technology (BCG, 1,000 executives in 59 countries). RAND's August 2024 study found the most common cause of failure is that people "misunderstand, or miscommunicate, what problem needs to be solved using AI" (RAND, 65 interviews). A consultant who starts with the tool, not the problem, walks straight into that. We cover the pattern in more depth in why AI projects fail.

Which questions should you ask an AI consultant?

Ask these five questions in the first call, and ask for the answers in writing. A genuine adviser will answer each one in a sentence.

  1. Do you sell, resell or build any AI tools? If yes, their advice will tend to lead to their own product, even with the best intentions.
  2. Do you take commissions, referral fees or kickbacks from any provider? Many software companies pay partners for each customer they bring. Ask directly; it is a normal question.
  3. Are you a partner or reseller of any provider? Partner badges look reassuring on a website. They also mean a commercial tie.
  4. Will you come on site and watch the work being done? A recommendation made from a questionnaire or a single workshop is a guess about your business.
  5. Will you tell us where not to use AI? Ask for an example of work they would leave alone, or would solve with plain automation instead.

Two follow-up questions are worth asking too: what you get at the end, and what happens to the notes they take about your business. You can see how we answer all of these on our AI Readiness Assessment questions.

What are the red flags?

The clearest red flag is a recommendation made before anyone has seen your work. Watch for these in Dubai, Abu Dhabi, Riyadh or anywhere else in the Gulf:

  • A free AI audit that leads into their own build work. The audit is the sales step, and the answer is usually the product or project they sell.
  • Commissions or referral fees. If they earn from the tool you buy, they are not neutral about which tool you buy.
  • Partner or reseller ties. A wall of supplier logos tells you who pays them, as well as who trained them.
  • A tool-first pitch. If the first meeting is a demonstration of a product, the conclusion was decided before the question. Tools chosen from a demo, without seeing the real work, tend to end up unused.
  • No site visit. An AI audit delivered in a day or two, without anyone coming to your office, has not seen how your people really work.
  • A readiness quiz sold as an assessment. An online quiz scores your own answers about yourself. It cannot see the hand-offs, waiting and rework that decide where AI helps.
  • Savings promised before the visit. Nobody can promise hours saved before they have timed the work.
  • No written quote. If the price is vague at the start, it rarely gets smaller later.

None of these prove bad intent. They tell you whose interests the advice is built around. The difference between a task that needs AI and one that needs a simple rule is often the first thing a seller blurs; our guide to AI vs automation explains it plainly.

Should you hire an adviser or a company that builds AI?

Hire an adviser to decide what to do, and a builder to do it, ideally as two separate decisions. A company that builds AI systems is often good at building. Its view of what you need will still lean towards what it builds.

An independent adviser has nothing to gain from any answer, so "automate this with a simple form", "fix the process first" or "leave this team alone" are all possible outcomes. Once you know what to do and what it should cost, you can brief a builder or buy a product directly from its maker, with a clear scope they cannot quietly expand.

Before you spend on either, it helps to know how to judge the return. Our guide to working out AI ROI before you buy sets out the sums.

Common questions about choosing an AI consultant in the GCC

How do I find an AI consultant in Dubai or Saudi Arabia?

Start with a short list from referrals and search, then run the five questions above on a first call. Rule out anyone who sells tools, takes commissions or will not come on site. The ones left can be judged on method, written scope and price.

Do AI consultants take commissions?

Some do. Many software providers run partner and referral programmes that pay for each customer brought to them. Ask any consultant directly, and get the answer in writing. We take none: our only income is the fee you pay us.

What is the best AI consulting for small businesses?

There is no single best; the right fit is an independent adviser who looks at your actual work and gives you a fixed price. For a business of twenty to a hundred people, a focused on-site assessment of the teams that matter most is usually more useful than a broad strategy document.

Is a free AI audit worth it?

It can be a useful conversation, but check what follows it. If the "audit" leads straight into the provider's own build work or product, treat it as a sales meeting. A real assessment means someone watching and timing the work on site, which takes days, not minutes.
